TITLETURBINE BLADE DEFECT TRIAGE — DETAIL SET
DWG 002.0

Why

Deep Learning · Sem V · evaluated 23 Nov 2026

An inspection drone comes back with hundreds of blade photos. Somebody has to decide which ones get a crew, which crew, and how soon.

That's triage. The damage type decides who goes up (crack, erosion and lightning damage need different people and kit), and the severity decides when. So the model answers two questions per photo, not one.

It started as a semester project with a two-day build target. The version on this page is the one I audited afterwards: every number recomputed from saved checkpoints, the claims cut back to what the test set supports, and the failures kept in.

DWG 002.1

Pipeline

Figshare XML → crew + priority

Seven stages, and only one of them is the neural network.

Most of the work, and most of the ways to fool yourself, sit before training: how the labels are made and how the frames are split. Click a stage for what happens there.

DWG 002.2

Labels

REQ-02 · my proxy rule, not the dataset's

The dataset has boxes and class names. It has no severity at all, so I wrote a rule.

Each class gets a base level (surface damage 1, erosion 2, craze cracking 3, crack and delamination 4, lightning 5). A box covering at least 8% of the image bumps it up one; a box under 0.5% knocks it down one. An image with several defects takes its worst one.

That rule changes the label on 473 of 1,065 images. It's loosely inspired by maintenance practice and it is not an industry standard, so every severity number on this site measures agreement with my rule, not real-world damage.

The rule also has a flaw I only saw later: box area in pixels depends on how far away the drone was. Pull the drone back and watch.

DWG 002.3

The split

REQ-03 · leak-free by sequence

The part that mattered most. Consecutive frames are often the same blade a second later.

Shuffle images into train and test and the model gets graded on near-copies of photos it trained on. The score looks great and means very little.

So frames are grouped first: a new group starts whenever the frame number jumps, or when the mean pixel difference to the previous frame (both shrunk to 64 × 64) goes above 75. That gives 361 groups, from 174 single frames up to one run of 27, and whole groups go to train, validation or test.

Every cell on the right is one of the 1,065 real frames, in order. Flip to a per-image shuffle and count the leaks.

DWG 002.4

Architecture

REQ-01 · ResNet50 · CBAM · two heads

A pretrained ResNet50 does the seeing. Everything after it is small.

The photo is resized to 224 × 224 and runs through all four ResNet stages, ending as a 2048 × 7 × 7 feature map. CBAM then re-weights it twice: which channels matter, then where in the 7 × 7 grid. Average and max pooling are concatenated into 4,096 numbers, squeezed to 512, and three heads read that.

The type head is trained with class-weighted focal loss (γ = 2) so rare classes like lightning still count. Severity is ordinal: four yes/no thresholds ("worse than L1? worse than L2? …"), and the level is one plus the number of yeses. A scalar regression head rides along as an extra signal.

L = focal(type) + 0.5 · SmoothL1(scalar) + 0.5 · BCE(4 thresholds)

The whole network is fine-tuned end to end: AdamW at 3e-4, cosine down to 1e-6, 12 epochs, batch 16, seed 42. About five minutes on a laptop RTX 3050.

DWG 002.5

Ablation

Same code · same split · same seed

Deep features beat hand-crafted ones by a mile. Past that, the differences are a handful of images.

Four runs on the same 159 test images. A colour-and-edge SVM gets 36%. A plain ResNet50 gets 93.7%. Adding CBAM gets 94.3%, one more image right. The full multi-task model C gets 92.5%, two fewer.

To ask whether C really differs from A, only the images where exactly one of them is right matter. A alone gets 4 of those, C alone gets 2. If the two models were equally good, a 4–2 split would happen by coin flip 69% of the time. So: not significant, and a single seed can't settle it.

Drag the counts to see how lopsided it would need to be.

DWG 002.6

Severity

REQ-02 · model C · ordinal head

The fair question for a severity head: is it better than just looking up the class?

Because the labels come from class + box size, a lookup table is a real competitor. Even a lookup that's handed the true class, which no deployed system would have, is off by 0.415 levels on average. The learned head is off by 0.346 and exactly right 71% of the time, so it has picked up the size part of the rule too.

The matrix is where it gets less comfortable. The most common mistake is calling a level 5 a level 4, sixteen times. Three images are under-called by two or more levels, and those are the dangerous ones in triage.

DWG 002.7

Failures

REQ-04 · all 12 of model C's misses

Twelve wrong out of 159. Every one is below, with what the model was looking at.

Lightning damage is the weakest class: 4 of 15 missed. Burn marks with cracked edges look a lot like a craze network, and "craze crack" soaks up 7 of the 12 wrong answers. Images 14, 77, 95, 754 and 764 are wrong for all three deep models, so they're hard images, not one model's quirk.

Grad-CAM doesn't always flatter it either. On 729 the heat sits on a gantry in the background, not the blade, and on 539 a second hot spot lands on rocks on the ground.

Most misses are also unsure ones. If the system handed anything under a confidence threshold to a person, how many mistakes would it catch, and how many good calls would it waste a human's time on? That rule isn't in the trained system; this is me working out whether it should be.

DWG 002.8

Limits

REQ-05

It can't tell you a blade is healthy. Every photo it ever saw had damage in it.

All 1,065 images contain at least one defect, so the model has six answers and no "none of the above". Its probabilities always add up to one across those six. On 23 November I showed it a photo of a brand-new blade and it said crack.

That's why it's scoped as triage of damage that has already been reported, and why the farm on the main sheet only sends the drone to turbines whose output has dropped. A real deployment needs a first stage that says "this looks normal" before this model is asked anything.

The other limits are smaller but real: severity is measured against my own rule (002.2), apparent size changes with drone distance, and one seed on 159 test images can't separate models that differ by one or two images (002.5).

DWG 002.R

Revisions

What I'd build next

The next version is mostly about what happens before this model, not inside it.

A first-stage anomaly detector would let it see healthy blades. A fixed capture distance, or ground sample distance from the drone's altitude, would turn pixel area into real area. Measuring damage against the blade's own width in the same photo would make size distance-proof, but needs a blade segmentation model first.

And before claiming CBAM or multi-task learning helps, more seeds and a bigger test set. Right now the honest answer is "can't tell".
