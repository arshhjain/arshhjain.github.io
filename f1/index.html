DWG 003.0

Why

FastF1 · 2018–2024 · 131 races · 28 circuits

Everyone knows Monaco is a procession and Bahrain usually isn't. I wanted to know how much of that is the track itself.

Two questions: which circuits actually produce on-track racing, and can you tell from the layout alone, before a race has ever been run there?

The second question is the useful one. Circuit designers and organisers argue about straights, DRS zones and street layouts all the time; a model that ranks circuits it has never seen would say which of those things matter.

DWG 003.1

A pass

REQ-01 · running order at the end of every lap

A pass is two cars swapping places between one lap and the next, on track.

FastF1 gives the running order at the end of every lap. V1 summed every place gained and halved it. That also counts a car rejoining from the pits behind you, a rival pitting in front of you, and everyone moving up one when someone retires.

V2 looks at pairs instead. A pair counts when their order flips from lap n−1 to lap n, neither car pitted on either lap, and the track was green on both (no safety car, VSC or red flag). A retirement never counts, because the retired car has no position to swap.

DWG 003.2

Fake races

REQ-02 · checked against FastF1's own calendar

Ask FastF1 for a race that didn't happen, and it quietly gives you a different one.

The fetch script asked for every circuit's race in every season. When a Grand Prix wasn't on that year's calendar, FastF1's fuzzy name matching picked the closest event it could find and loaded that instead, with a warning nobody reads in a loop of 200 requests.

So "Australia 2021", a race cancelled for COVID, was sitting in my data as a real race. Nine like it, plus Spa 2021 (three laps behind the safety car, then called off). V2 checks every race against the season calendar and drops all ten.

DWG 003.3

Pit stops

REQ-03 · the confounder

V1's count rose and fell with the number of pit stops. It was measuring strategy, not racing.

Every pit stop drops a car down the order and then sends it back up as the others stop, and V1 counted all of that as overtaking. More stops, more "overtakes". That's why high tyre-wear tracks like Barcelona and Bahrain sat near the top.

The clearest case is Qatar 2023, where the FIA capped tyre stints at 18 laps and forced three stops each. V1 scored it 178, so I dropped it as an anomaly. V2 scores it 44, a perfectly normal race. The anomaly was the metric.

Counting real passes cuts the link with pit stops from a correlation of 0.48 to 0.19. Some link should remain, since fresh tyres genuinely help cars pass.

DWG 003.4

The model

REQ-04 · ridge regression · leave one circuit out

One row per circuit, five layout features, and every circuit is predicted with itself hidden.

Target: each race's passes per green lap, divided by that season's median race, then averaged per circuit. 1.0 means a typical race. Rows are weighted by how many races a circuit has.

Features: track length, longest straight (from telemetry), corners per km, DRS zones and street-or-permanent, all checked against official circuit data. Where the layout changed mid-window, the layout most of the races used.

Testing: 28 circuits is tiny, so a random split would be noise, and V1's random row split let a circuit's other seasons leak into training. Here the model is refit 28 times, each time without one circuit, and predicts that circuit. Ridge's strength is picked inside each fold, so the held-out circuit never touches it.

DWG 003.5

Limits

REQ-05 · what it can't say

The circuit explains about 45% of how many passes a race gets. The other 55% is that particular race.

That's measured straight from the data: split the variation in pass rate between "which circuit" and "which race at that circuit". Rain, safety cars, a fast car starting at the back, a dominant leader: none of that is in a layout. So no layout-only model could ever predict single races well, and that's why this one predicts circuits.

DWG 003.R

Revisions

V1 → V2, and what I'd build next

V1 had a better-looking R² and a worse question. V2 fixed the target before touching the model.

V1 was a confidence-weighted random forest on four telemetry features predicting a "drama score": R² 0.17, MAE 0.128 on a 0–1 scale, on a random 80/20 split of rows, so other seasons of the same circuit sat in training. Running V2's model on V1's position-change count, tested circuit by circuit, scores about zero (R² −0.01, rank correlation 0.04). All of V2's gain came from counting the right thing.

Next is the feature I think is missing: track width at each braking zone. A pass needs a long run, a big stop and room to go side by side, so one "passing zone" score per braking point should beat five separate numbers. No dataset has the widths, so they'd be measured by hand from satellite images.
